Read All About It

How Australia's newspapers grew with every wave of settlement, peaked around the First World War, then closed faster than they opened — and why what we can read online today is only part of the story.

1 A young country finds its press

The first paper, Sydney's Gazette, appeared in 1803. For decades the colonies printed only a few thousand articles a year. Then people arrived, and the presses followed.

Trove holds 81,624 articles for 1850, 637,508 for 1880 and 2.6 million for 1900. Victoria's yearly total grew from about 19,000 articles in 1851 to almost 75,000 in 1859, in the first years of its gold rush. Western Australia's own gold rush shows up in the 1890s, when its yearly total rose from 114,000 (1895) to 359,000 (1898).

Read the chart as stacked bands. Each band's height is one state's yearly total, and the top edge is the national total. In 1840 that total was only 40,185 articles. By 1900 it was about 65 times larger.

2 The peak, and a trap in the data

Launches peaked in the 1890s, when 247 new titles appeared. The 1910s look even bigger (307), but look at the grey bars. 86 of those are Victorian local papers that Trove holds only for the years 1914–1918. They are slices of papers that ran for longer, not new launches or closures, so they make the 1910s look busier than they were.

Set the grey bars aside and a clearer pattern remains. From the 1920s more titles close than open: a net loss of 32 in the 1920s, 38 in the 1930s and 47 in the 1940s.

The same bulge shows up in the article counts of Chapter 1. Victoria's yearly total jumps from 766,000 articles in 1913 to 1.28 million in 1914, then drops to 509,000 by 1919, which is why Trove's national count appears to “peak” at 4.8 million in 1915. In fact the 1920s and 1930s are just as high (4.7 million in 1929). The honest reading is a long plateau from about 1914 to 1939, followed by a decline.

Papers built to last

A few titles survived for a century or more. Echuca's Riverine Herald was printed for 134 years (1869–2002), and Sydney's French-language Le Courrier Australien for 120. The big-city dailies — The Sydney Morning Herald, The Argus, The Age — anchor the list, along with country papers like the Border Watch in Mount Gambier.

Seven of the 15 began before 1860, and the oldest, the Sydney Morning Herald, started in 1842. Victoria supplies seven titles and New South Wales four. South Australia has three and Tasmania one. Queensland and Western Australia have none: in 1850 they had only one and two titles, and their presses grew later.

Only six of the 15 were printed in a capital city. The rest came from towns like Echuca, Beechworth and Mount Gambier, so a local paper could last as long as a big daily.

A run that ends in 1954 may have lasted longer in print, because Trove's coverage stops there.

3 Where the presses ran

Before 1860, three in four titles started in a capital city (158 of the 210 that we can place on a map). From 1860 to 1899 the pattern flips: 77% of new titles started in a regional town, as the goldfields, farming districts and railways created new communities that wanted their own paper. After 1900 the balance barely moved, with 74% of new titles still starting outside the capitals. In other words, the press spread out early and stayed spread out, and the three maps below show where.

Shading shows titles per 100,000 km², so states of very different sizes can be compared. Light grey means no data: no titles were in print there in the years you chose.

Why divide by area? Big states have more titles simply because they are big, so dividing by area makes the comparison fair. Over all years, Victoria has 200 titles per 100,000 km² and Tasmania 168, while Western Australia has only 15 and Queensland 6, even though Western Australia had the third-most titles overall (370). The Australian Capital Territory scores highest (339) because it is so small that it is hard to see on this map.

Titles with no place recorded (130 of 1,831) are left off, and each paper is placed at a single town.

The country is divided into squares 200 km wide, all the same size. The darker the square, the more titles were in print inside it during the years you chose.

Where towns sit close together, circles pile on top of each other. Squares add them up, so you can see where the press was thickest. With all years chosen, the three busiest squares are the Sydney (284 titles), Melbourne (174) and Perth (139) regions.

Sydney printed the most titles (174), followed by Perth (99), Melbourne (76), Hobart (70) and Adelaide (64). Western Australia punches above its weight. Its gold rushes of the 1890s helped it climb from 6th in 1870 to 2nd in 1930 in the number of papers in print, ahead of Victoria and South Australia. Six of the 15 towns with the most titles are in Western Australia: Perth, Fremantle, Geraldton, Coolgardie, Kalgoorlie and Bunbury.

The top five towns hold 483 of the 632 titles in this list. Sydney alone has 174, almost as many as Perth and Melbourne together (175). Brisbane, a capital city, ranks only seventh, with 22 titles.

Victoria and New South Wales swapped first place until 1890. From 1900, New South Wales stays on top. Tasmania, second in 1850, was last by 1880. South Australia ranked third from 1850 to 1890, and Queensland fourth from 1870 to 1890.

Western Australia's climb came in two steps. It had 13 titles in 1890 and 41 in 1900, then 91 by 1930, when it passed Victoria (65). Tasmania stayed last from 1880 onwards.

4 The press thins out

Indexing each state to its own 1929 total makes the decline easy to compare. Across Australia, preserved articles dipped to 88.5 in 1931, the Depression, then fell to 54.4 in 1943, the lowest point of the war.

South Australia (37.0) and Queensland (37.6) fell furthest by 1943, while New South Wales held up best (69.9). Victoria, which had the big 1914–1918 bulge, sits in the middle (50.4).

Read this carefully: the fall combines real closures with the way Trove has chosen which papers and years to digitise. New South Wales, Victoria and Western Australia dominate because that is what Trove holds; the Northern Territory and the Australian Capital Territory barely appear.

Between 1900 and 1930 the balance moved west. Western Australia more than doubled its titles in print (41 to 91), while Victoria fell from 110 to 65.

Each row is a state. The grey dot shows how many titles were in print in 1900 and the teal dot shows 1930, so a longer line means a bigger change. We chose these two years because they sit either side of Federation, the First World War and the point when closures began to outnumber launches.

New South Wales stayed far ahead of the others (155, then 163). Queensland and South Australia grew a little, and Tasmania barely moved (9 to 8), so its two dots sit on top of each other.

5 What we can read today

The press in other languages

The record is not only in English. In this list, 39 titles that began by 1954 were printed partly or wholly in 13 languages, led by German, Italian and Chinese. The German-language press goes back to 1848, and the Chinese-language press to the gold rushes of the 1850s.

German leads with 9 titles, ahead of Italian (8) and Chinese (7). Together those three make up 24 of the 39. Lithuanian, Estonian, Ukrainian and Polish papers all start between 1948 and 1950.

It covers only the titles in this dataset. The detection runs on text that a computer read from scanned pages.

A cliff in the record

Trove has 3.7 million articles for 1954 and only 436,000 for 1955, a drop of 88%. This is not newspapers vanishing: it is the line where copyright and permission limit what the library could digitise. Everything on this page should be read with that in mind.

After 1955 the yearly total never rises above 436,398. By 2020 it is down to about 3,200, because Trove holds only the few papers it has permission to show. Readers have also improved what is there: volunteers have corrected the machine-read text of about 16 million articles, roughly 6.5% of the total.

The record is still growing. Trove added between 117 and 322 newspaper titles to its list every year from 2011 to 2021, peaking at 322 in 2013. What looks like “the history of the press” keeps changing as more papers are digitised.

Each square is one state in one year. The darker the square, the more titles were added to Trove's list that year. Read across a row to follow one state, or down a column to compare states in the same year.

The states peaked at different times. New South Wales (183) and Victoria (105) peaked in 2013, but Tasmania peaked in 2017 (41) and Western Australia in 2020 (110). This suggests that papers were added state by state, not all at once.

“Other / unknown” means no state was recorded for the title (84 titles in all).

About this story

How this story was made

1 Collect

Trove's lists of titles (start and end years, state and place) and its yearly article counts, by state, came from the GLAM Workbench. State boundaries and areas came from the Australian Bureau of Statistics.

2 Clean and join

We kept titles with both a start and an end year, and matched each title to the first town listed for it. Victorian papers that Trove holds only for 1914–1918 are flagged as separate slices.

3 Calculate

A title is in print in every year between its start and end. Titles per 100,000 km² is titles divided by state area, times 100,000. Each article count is indexed to its own 1929 total (1929 = 100).

4 Draw

Every chart is written in Vega-Lite. The maps share one equal-area projection, and the squares on the square map are all 200 km wide. One year selector drives the town, state and square maps.

Limits of the data

  • Trove holds only what has been digitised, mostly papers published before 1955.
  • Counts are articles digitised, not everything that was printed.
  • End dates around 1954 are an effect of the collection.
  • Dates are the runs recorded in Trove. They can differ from a paper's full printing history.
  • Each title is placed at one location, so a paper serving a whole district appears at a single town.
  • Titles without a recorded place are left off the town and square maps, but included in the state map.
  • State is read from the place recorded for each title. Titles without one are grouped as “Other / unknown”.
  • A small share of articles has no state recorded, so the state totals add up to slightly less than the national total. In 1954 the states add up to 3.63 million against 3.66 million overall (about 1%). In no year from 1850 to 1954 is the gap more than about 2.5%.
